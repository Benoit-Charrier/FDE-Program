### Q7: Does Availity tell you when a patient's insurance is about to expire, or do you only find out when you try to verify and it fails?

**Dana's Answer**:

"Availity doesn't give proactive alerts. We only find out when we query the system — either during a scheduled verification before a visit, or when a claim gets rejected after the visit.

So yeah, it's reactive, not proactive. We find out when we check, or when it's already too late and billing gets a denial.

That's why my 6-month re-verification rule is important. If we check patients every 6 months — especially chronic patients who come regularly — we catch expirations or plan changes before claims get filed. But right now, that's on me to remember. Front-desk doesn't track it."

### Q11: What keywords or phrases in a patient's visit reason automatically make you think 'I need to tell the doctor before this visit'?

**Dana's Answer**:

"Okay, here's my mental list of 'always escalate' keywords:

**Cardiac/Respiratory**:
- Chest pain
- Shortness of breath / difficulty breathing / can't catch my breath
- Heart palpitations / racing heart

**Neurological**:
- Severe headache / worst headache of my life
- Vision changes / blurry vision / seeing double
- Numbness or tingling (especially one side of body)
- Dizziness / loss of balance / fainting

**Severe Pain**:
- Severe abdominal pain
- Severe pain (any location) with 'sudden onset' or 'can't move'

**Bleeding/Trauma**:
- Bleeding (not minor cuts — like 'bleeding that won't stop' or 'blood in stool/urine/vomit')
- Fall with head injury
- Injury with swelling/inability to move limb

**Acute Change**:
- Sudden onset (anything that started suddenly, not gradually)
- Can't walk / can't stand / can't move [body part]

**Pregnancy-related** (we don't do OB, but if a patient mentions it):
- Any pregnancy complication (bleeding, severe pain, no fetal movement)

If a patient's visit reason contains any of these, I want front-desk to flag it. Then I (or the doctor) call the patient before the visit to assess whether they should go to the ER, come in immediately as same-day urgent, or if it's okay to wait for their scheduled appointment.

The tricky ones are vague symptoms like 'not feeling well,' 'tired,' 'pain' (without the word 'severe'). Those could be anything. I usually tell front-desk: **if the patient uses a word that implies severity ('severe,' 'can't,' 'sudden,' 'worst') or if it's cardiac/respiratory/neuro, flag it. Otherwise, document it as routine and the doctor will assess at the visit.**

But that's informal training. We don't have a written protocol. It's just what I've told them over the years."

### Q13: What does 'no clinical judgment' mean to you in practice? Where's the line between administrative triage and clinical triage?

**Dana's Answer**:

"Good question. I think about it this way:

**Administrative triage** (safe for front-desk and AI):
- Recognizing urgent keywords ('chest pain,' 'shortness of breath,' 'sudden severe headache')
- Flagging those for clinician review before the visit
- Asking basic screening questions ('How long have you had this symptom? Is it getting worse?')
- Escalating when the patient's language suggests urgency

**Clinical triage** (requires RN, MD, or PA):
- Deciding *how* urgent something is (ER now vs. same-day visit vs. scheduled appointment)
- Interpreting symptoms in context (is 'chest pain' in this patient likely cardiac, GI, musculoskeletal?)
- Determining whether symptoms require immediate medical attention
- Giving medical advice ('You should take aspirin and call 911')

Front-desk should flag potential urgency. They should NOT assess severity or decide on disposition.

Example:
- ✅ **Administrative triage (okay for front-desk/AI)**: Patient writes 'chest pain.' Front-desk flags it and says, 'This patient reported chest pain. I flagged it for you to review.'
- ❌ **Clinical triage (NOT okay for front-desk/AI)**: Patient writes 'chest pain.' Front-desk says, 'This patient reported chest pain, but it's probably just indigestion because they also mentioned heartburn, so I marked it routine.'

Front-desk can recognize keywords and escalate. They cannot assess severity, rule out serious causes, or make a judgment about what's safe to wait for.

That's the line. **Recognition → escalate. Assessment → clinician.**

For an AI agent, I'd want it to work the same way. Agent sees 'chest pain' → flags for Dana or doctor to review. Agent does NOT say 'this chest pain is low-risk' or 'this patient can wait.' Even if the agent is really smart, I don't want it making clinical judgments. Too much liability, and honestly, I wouldn't trust it for something that could be life-threatening.

But flagging urgent keywords and escalating ambiguous cases? That's helpful. That's what front-desk should be doing anyway, and an agent could standardize it."

## Category D: Medication Reconciliation

### Q14: You mentioned DoseSpot misses things. What are the most common medication sources that DoseSpot doesn't capture? Other providers' prescriptions, OTC meds, supplements, something else?

**Dana's Answer**:

"All of the above, honestly. DoseSpot is pretty good at capturing prescriptions filled at major pharmacy chains — CVS, Walgreens, Rite Aid, the ones in our network. But here's what it misses:

**1. Out-of-network pharmacies** (maybe 10-15% of our patients):
- Patient uses a mail-order pharmacy from their insurance (like Express Scripts, Optum Rx)
- Patient fills at a small independent pharmacy not in the network
- Patient travels and fills prescriptions out of state
- Patient uses a pharmacy chain that doesn't share data with DoseSpot (rare, but happens)

**2. Other providers' prescriptions** (very common):
- Specialists prescribe medications that our doctors don't know about (cardiologist adds a beta blocker, endocrinologist adjusts thyroid med, psychiatrist prescribes an antidepressant)
- ER or urgent care prescribes antibiotics or pain meds
- Even if those prescriptions are filled at a network pharmacy, DoseSpot shows the fill, but we don't always know *who* prescribed it or *why*. The patient says, 'Oh yeah, my cardiologist started that,' but we have no record of it.

**3. Over-the-counter (OTC) medications and supplements**:
- Aspirin, ibuprofen, acetaminophen (Tylenol), allergy meds (Claritin, Zyrtec)
- Vitamins, herbal supplements (St. John's wort, ginkgo, fish oil)
- These are huge interaction risks — like, patients on warfarin (blood thinner) taking ibuprofen or fish oil, which increases bleeding risk. But they don't think to mention it because 'it's just over-the-counter.'

**4. Medication samples from doctors**:
- Specialists give out sample packs of medications (especially expensive ones like inhalers, diabetes meds, name-brand drugs)
- Patient starts taking it, but there's no pharmacy fill, so DoseSpot never sees it

**5. Medications the patient stopped taking but are still listed**:
- DoseSpot shows a fill from 3 months ago, patient says 'Oh, I stopped that,' but they never told us, so it's still in athenahealth's med list
- Or the patient ran out, didn't refill, but our system still shows it as active

The biggest issue is that **DoseSpot is a pharmacy fill history, not a comprehensive medication list**. If it's not filled at a pharmacy we can see, or if it's OTC, or if it's a sample, DoseSpot doesn't know about it. And patients forget to tell us.

So when we do medication reconciliation at check-in, front-desk asks: 'Are you taking any medications not on this list?' But patients say 'no' because they forget, or they don't think to mention OTC meds, or they assume we already know about the cardiologist's new prescription.

The doctor usually catches discrepancies during the visit when they review the med list again and probe more. But by then, we've wasted front-desk time on an incomplete reconciliation."

### Q15: When a patient says 'I'm on the same medications as last time,' how often is that actually true vs. they forgot something changed?

**Dana's Answer**:

*Dana laughs.*

"Maybe 60-70% of the time it's actually true. The other 30-40%, something changed and they either forgot or didn't think it was important to mention.

Common scenarios where 'same as last time' is wrong:

**1. Specialist changed a dose or added something** (20-30%):
- Cardiologist increased their blood pressure med from 10mg to 20mg
- Endocrinologist adjusted thyroid medication
- Psychiatrist added an antidepressant
- Patient thinks, 'It's the same medication, just a different dose,' so they say 'same as last time.' But dose changes matter!

**2. Patient stopped taking something** (10-15%):
- They ran out and didn't refill (often because of cost)
- They had a side effect and stopped on their own (didn't tell anyone)
- They 'felt better' and decided they didn't need it anymore (especially antibiotics, blood pressure meds)

**3. Patient started an OTC med** (10%):
- Daily aspirin for heart health
- Allergy medication for seasonal allergies
- Pain reliever for arthritis
- They don't think to mention it because 'it's not a prescription'

**4. Patient is taking someone else's medication** (rare, but happens):
- Spouse's medication ('We have the same condition, so I take his pills when I run out')
- Borrowed pain meds from a family member

So when front-desk asks 'same as last time?' and the patient says yes, front-desk documents 'no changes' and moves on. Then the doctor reviews the list during the visit, asks more specific questions — 'Are you still taking the lisinopril? Any new medications from other doctors? Any over-the-counter meds?' — and suddenly three things come up that weren't mentioned at check-in.

It's frustrating because we spend 6 minutes at check-in doing a reconciliation that turns out to be incomplete, and then the doctor spends another 2-3 minutes redoing it. Ideally, we'd catch everything at check-in, but patients just aren't reliable reporters.

If an AI agent could compare the DoseSpot list, the athenahealth list, and the patient's verbal report, then flag discrepancies for the doctor to review, that would be better. Like: 'DoseSpot shows a fill for atorvastatin 20mg on 10/15, but athenahealth lists atorvastatin 10mg. Patient said no changes. Please verify with patient.' Then the doctor knows exactly what to ask about."

### Q16: Have there been cases where a medication discrepancy led to a clinical issue — like the doctor prescribed something that interacted with a med we didn't know about?

**Dana's Answer**:

"Yes. Not often, but it has happened, and it's scary when it does.

One case about a year ago: Patient didn't tell us they'd been prescribed warfarin (blood thinner) by their cardiologist. We didn't have it in our system because the cardiologist uses a different EHR, and the patient filled the prescription at a mail-order pharmacy that DoseSpot didn't capture.

Our doctor prescribed an antibiotic — I think it was sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (Bactrim) — for a UTI. That antibiotic interacts with warfarin and can increase bleeding risk. Pharmacist caught it and called us. We had to switch antibiotics.

That could've been bad. If the pharmacist hadn't caught it, the patient's INR (blood clotting measure) could've gone dangerously high, and they could've had a major bleed.

Another case: Patient was taking St. John's wort (herbal supplement for depression) and didn't mention it because 'it's just a supplement.' Doctor prescribed an SSRI antidepressant. Both together can cause serotonin syndrome, which is serious. Pharmacist caught that one too.

So yes, it's happened. Not frequently — maybe 2-3 times a year that I know of where there was a significant interaction risk. But 2-3 times a year is 2-3 times too many, because those are preventable.

The issue is, we rely on the patient to tell us about medications from other providers, OTC meds, and supplements. And they forget, or they don't think it matters. If we had a better way to cross-check — like, pulling fill history from all pharmacies, not just our network, or prompting the patient specifically about OTC and supplements — we'd catch more of these.

Medication reconciliation is high-stakes. An error there can literally kill someone. So yeah, it's a priority. And if an AI agent can help catch discrepancies that front-desk misses, that's a huge safety win."

### Q17: Does DoseSpot show medication fills from other states or other providers? Or only from pharmacies your practice typically uses?

**Dana's Answer**:

"DoseSpot has pretty good coverage for fills in our state (Pennsylvania) and neighboring states (Maryland, Delaware, New Jersey), at least for the major chains. If a patient fills at CVS in Pennsylvania or CVS in Maryland, DoseSpot usually picks it up.

But:
- **Small independent pharmacies**: Hit or miss. Some share data with DoseSpot, some don't.
- **Out of state beyond neighboring states**: If a patient travels to Florida or California and fills a prescription there, DoseSpot might not see it, especially if it's at a regional chain that doesn't operate in the mid-Atlantic.
- **Mail-order pharmacies**: Some yes, some no. Express Scripts shows up sometimes. Optum Rx is spotty. Amazon Pharmacy I haven't seen yet, but we don't have many patients using it.
- **Other providers**: DoseSpot shows the fill, but not the prescriber details. Like, it'll say 'atorvastatin 20mg filled at CVS on 10/15,' but it won't say 'prescribed by Dr. Smith, cardiologist.' So we know the patient is taking it, but we don't always know why or who prescribed it unless the patient tells us.

So DoseSpot is better than nothing — it's way better than asking patients to remember everything — but it's not comprehensive. I'd say we capture maybe 70-80% of pharmacy fills, and 0% of OTC, samples, or borrowed medications.

For an AI agent, I think the right approach is:
1. Pull DoseSpot fill history (captures most pharmacy fills)
2. Compare to athenahealth med list (captures what we've documented from past visits)
3. Flag discrepancies
4. **Explicitly prompt the patient** with questions like:
   - 'Do you take any medications filled at other pharmacies or out of state?'
   - 'Do you take any over-the-counter medications like aspirin, ibuprofen, or allergy meds?'
   - 'Do you take any vitamins or herbal supplements?'
   - 'Have any of your other doctors (specialists, urgent care, ER) prescribed new medications?'

That way, we're not relying on the patient to volunteer information. We're actively asking."

### Q21: What patient populations don't fit the standard intake flow? For example, non-English speakers, patients without portal access, patients who always call instead of filling out forms?

**Dana's Answer**:

"Good question. We have a few non-standard groups:

**1. Non-English speakers (maybe 5-10% of our patients)**:

- Pre-visit questionnaires are in English, which is a problem. Non-English speakers either skip the questionnaire, or a family member fills it out for them, which means the information might not be accurate.

**2. Patients without portal access (maybe 20-30%)**:

- If an AI agent is parsing visit reasons, it needs to handle both online questionnaire responses and phone intake notes.

**3. Patients who always call or walk in (10-15%)**:
- Some patients just prefer calling or walking in instead of using the portal. They're usually older or less tech-savvy.
- We accommodate them, but it's less efficient because front-desk has to do manual intake on the spot.

**4. Complex patients with multiple chronic conditions (maybe 10-15%)**:
- Patients with diabetes + heart disease + kidney disease + hypertension — their medication lists are 10-15 drugs long, they see 4-5 specialists, and reconciliation takes forever.
- DoseSpot shows a ton of fills, athenahealth has a huge med list, and the patient can't always remember who prescribed what or why.

So the standard intake flow works for most patients — probably 70-80%. But 20-30% have some variation that needs special handling.

For an AI system, I'd want it to:
- Detect language preference (if the patient's demographic info says 'Spanish,' flag for bilingual staff)
- Handle both structured questionnaire data and unstructured phone notes
- Recognize complex patients and flag them for extra review time
- Have an easy escalation path for 'this patient doesn't fit the standard flow, needs human review.'"
